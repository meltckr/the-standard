Throughout my career, I have heard the same description of certain players and coaches: He is a great leader because he leads by example.

I understand why. Example matters. People notice who prepares, who keeps his word, and who remains dependable when the work becomes uncomfortable. Without that credibility, nobody has earned the right to ask more of a teammate.

But meeting the standard is still doing your job. It proves that you can lead yourself. It does not prove that you will protect the team when someone beside you stops meeting it.

Dwyane Wade recently described the strongest leaders he encountered during his Hall of Fame career. What he said about Pat Riley and Udonis Haslem reveals an important division of responsibility inside the Miami Heat.

Riley established the standard. Wade remembered that details were corrected in practice whether the mistake came from the first unit or the third. The expectation did not change with status.

Haslem carried that standard into the locker room. He was never Miami’s biggest star, but Wade said he had its strongest voice because it was not being used selfishly. Haslem made sure Riley’s rules applied to everybody. When LeBron James and Chris Bosh arrived, that did not change.

Wade put the sequence clearly: accountability to each other starts with accountability individually.

Haslem earned the right to speak through the way he served, prepared, and competed. But his leadership did not stop with his own example. He used that trust to protect the standard in somebody else, even when that person had more talent or status.

That is the distinction. Your example protects your credibility. Your voice protects the team.

Every coach has seen what happens when that voice is missing. A player gives less than the required effort. A detail goes uncorrected. Everybody sees it, but each person assumes somebody else will handle it, or decides that doing his own job is enough.

The first silence buys comfort. The next creates precedent. Soon the team has two standards: the one posted on the wall and the one people have learned they can get away with.

Silence can come from uncertainty about role, fear of damaging a relationship, or the assumption that a formal leader will intervene. But the group still receives a message: this behavior is survivable here.

Team scientists identify mutual performance monitoring and backup behavior as core parts of teamwork. Effective teammates notice lapses, communicate what they see, and help one another recover before the shared work breaks down.

Other research has shown that visible rule-breaking can make additional violations more likely. The practical lesson is simple: people learn the real standard from what they see being tolerated, not only from what a leader announces.

This separates a working group from a team. A working group can be built around individual responsibility. A team requires individual and mutual responsibility for a shared result. The unit changes when people accept that the work beside them is also their concern.

The same principle appears in business. At Pixar, Ed Catmull helped build the Braintrust around candid peer review. Filmmakers put unfinished work in front of respected colleagues expected to identify problems honestly. The discussions could be intense, but not personal. The group could not seize the project or dictate the answer. Its responsibility was to tell the truth so the owner could make the work better.

Formal leaders define the standard, model it, teach it, and support people who speak honestly. But they cannot be present in every huddle, hallway, meeting, and decision. If the standard exists only when authority is in the room, it is not yet culture. It is supervision.

There is an important difference between confrontation and aggression. Useful accountability protects the dignity of the teammate and the integrity of the work. It identifies an observable behavior, connects it to a shared expectation, and provides a clear next action.

The goal is not to prove who is right or to win the confrontation. The goal is to restore the standard while preserving the relationship required to pursue it together.

That means earning the right to speak. Saying it early, while the issue is still about an action. Addressing the person directly, with the smallest audience that can solve the problem. Naming what must change, confirming the next action, and recognizing the correction when it happens.

Accountability without a path back becomes punishment. Leadership gives the person a way to return to the standard and remain part of the team.

So the question is not only who works hard, prepares well, and consistently meets expectations. The harder question is this: who are we calling a leader because he meets the standard, but has not shown that he will protect it in others?

Set the example. Confront the drift. Protect the team.
